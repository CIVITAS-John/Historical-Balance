# The Crown Across the Strait

## 1662–1683: The Zheng kingdom survives

Koxinga and Shi Lang settle their quarrel in 1651, and Shi stays in Zheng service. The Dutch still surrender on Taiwan in 1662. When Koxinga learns of the scandal in his son Zheng Jing’s household, he puts Jing under house arrest, spares his family, and has Feng Xifan executed as the scapegoat. Koxinga lives long enough to lead an expedition to the Philippines. It wins some early successes around Manila Bay, but when he dies in 1663, the Zheng can neither take Manila nor be driven out cheaply. Chen Yonghua negotiates a withdrawal that lets more Chinese families and merchants resettle in Taiwan.

Zheng Jing succeeds his father. With the extra settlers and revenue, he holds Xiamen longer and gains more ground in Fujian when Wu Sangui leads the Revolt of the Three Feudatories. Those gains unravel as Qing forces recover. The withdrawal saves many more soldiers, merchants, and their families, and Taiwan becomes the kingdom’s permanent home.

Under Jing, dispatches to foreign courts go out in the name of Tungning (東寧). At home the Zheng still govern in the name of Great Ming (大明), dating their records by the Yongli reign even after the emperor’s death. The Zheng hold the seals of government; no Zhu sits on Taiwan’s throne.

When Jing dies in 1681, Feng is long dead and the palace coup never happens. Zheng Kezang inherits as Qing pressure mounts. Manila offers powder, supplies, and access to Spanish markets if the court embraces Catholicism. Kezang presents his conversion as the fulfillment of a promise made in the name of the Southern Ming’s Yongli court. Spain wants a reliable outlet for trade as Qing restrictions close other doors; the Zheng can supply porcelain, silk, and tea. With Spanish supplies, Shi Lang’s Zheng fleet repels Kangxi’s expedition in 1683. The Ming prince Zhu Shugui keeps his estates in Taiwan and presides over ceremonies honoring the old dynasty.

## 1683–1799: An island left off the record

Kangxi keeps the trade ban and builds up his coastal fleet. Yongzheng sees little reason to pay for another invasion. After the Qing defeat by the Dzungars at Hetongpo (Hoton Nor) in 1731, the northwest has first claim on his troops and treasury.

Qianlong wants Taiwan to count among the victories he will celebrate as his Ten Great Campaigns. He Shen persuades him to shelve the plan: landing troops is easier than keeping them supplied while the Zheng fleet can cut the crossing. Instead, He Shen quietly buys firearms through Tungning merchants and puts foreign gunsmiths in touch with Qing arsenals. Improved weapons serve the emperor on his other campaigns. The trade goes through private accounts, and Taiwan never appears in the official record of his victories.

Meanwhile, the Zheng court looks south to burnish its claim to be more than a Ming remnant. Its merchants and ships win tributary ties with small courts such as Sintang, Selangor, and Bulungan. Lanfang accepts Tungning protection and eventually comes under its administration. A license from the shogunate keeps trade with Nagasaki alive; a warehouse lease at Busan gives the kingdom a discreet foothold in Joseon.

## 1799–1836: An embargo with a porous shore

When Jiaqing has He Shen put to death in 1799, his intermediaries close their books. The arms trade ends. Jiaqing has rebellions closer to home, and Daoguang has fiscal troubles and mounting pressure over the opium trade. Both leave the island alone. In 1836, Qing still claims Taiwan, patrols the strait, and bars trade with the Zheng. The court has little appetite for another expedition.

Fishing boats and family ties outlast the official ban. Some Fujianese returning from Taiwan bring their Catholic faith home, where it takes root in a few coastal communities. The same networks help people find work overseas when unrest spreads or jobs dry up. Fujian has the closest links to Taiwan, Guangdong has its own longstanding routes into Southeast Asia, and departures from Zhejiang are less frequent. Where work is steady and living conditions are good, most families stay.

Across the strait, Zheng Wenhai inherits a kingdom built for busier times. The old 鄭 command banner still flies over Anping, where foreign ships clear customs under the name Tungning. Spain’s reach is shrinking. Dutch rivals and the East India Company take more of the shipping trade, while the Qing fleet has grown large enough to make the crossing dangerous. Taiwan still has shipyards, an arms industry, export workshops, and an overseas network. Paying for the fleet that protects them is another matter.

Shi Lang’s descendant Shi Jichang commands Wenhai’s fleet. Chen Zhaowen, descended from the man who negotiated the Manila withdrawal, speaks for civil servants calling for reform. Yang Jingyuan, whose ancestor Yang Ying kept Koxinga’s military accounts, speaks for merchants who have put their money into mills and trade houses in Taiwan and Busan. Zhu Yucheng, a cadet of the Ming imperial family, holds the title of Prince of Ningjing and lives on his family’s estates. His title is ceremonial, though some at court still look to the Zhu family for a ruler.

One branch of [Xu Guangqi’s Catholic family](https://www.ccsenet.org/journal/index.php/ach/article/download/42160/23054) settles in Taiwan under the Zheng. Its descendant Xu Wenjing now leads the court’s reformist scholars and presses the university to teach mathematics alongside the classics. Father Luo Jingde, a priest trained in Manila, speaks for the Catholic clergy. A few Siraya congregations keep the Reformed faith after Dutch rule ends. In Sinkan, the schoolmaster Samuel Talavan teaches from [Siraya texts written in the Latin alphabet](https://www.ling.sinica.edu.tw/item/en?act=publish_book&bookID=55&code=view) and asks Wenhai’s court to let his congregation print its own readers.

## The factions at Wenhai’s court

The **Ming Estates** (Landowners) hold sugar plantations and manor houses. The Zhu princes, other refugee Ming aristocrats, the landed branch of the Zheng family, and traditional Confucian teachers share a place here. They honor the dynasty that gave their families their titles. They also want to keep the estates they built in exile. Prince Zhu Yucheng’s circle wants a return to China; plantation families will hear out a bargain with Qing if the embargo is ruining them.

The **Maritime Houses** (Industrialists) are shipowners, exporters, manufacturers, and Zheng cousins with money in the ports. Yang Jingyuan traces their fortunes to the [armed trade and piracy of Zheng Zhilong and his son Koxinga](https://unora.unior.it/handle/11574/34968). The family remembers both legacies: the landed branch tells the story of Koxinga’s Ming commission, while the Maritime Houses look to Zhilong’s trading network. They want open ports and new settlements in Southeast Asia; they care about China as a market and source of migrants, and will resist pledging their profits to a dynastic war.

The **Catholic Missions** (Devout) are Father Luo’s clergy, schools, and lay patrons. They want to keep their churches and maintain the Manila connection. They make the case for Western books, medicine, and technical instruction because the missions already bring them across the sea. If the court finally adopts a different faith, another religious network takes the public place the missions held: the **Reformed Congregations**, the **Ritual Academies**, or the **Buddhist Temples**. The Catholics still have their churches and followers after losing court patronage.

The **School of Practical Learning** (Intelligentsia) reads the Confucian classics and expects scholars to test what they teach. Xu Wenjing draws on the example of his ancestor’s [translation of Euclid with Matteo Ricci](https://www.loc.gov/resource/lcnclscd.2014514121.1A005/?sp=2&st=list). He is Catholic and works with the missions, but wants mathematics, surveying, and schools to serve public offices beyond the reach of any one clergy. His students can supply commissioners for a mainland campaign, or make the case for a republic. The school’s arguments with the old estate tutors are arguments within Confucian learning as much as disputes about religion.

The **Army and Fleet** (Armed Forces) unite the island’s soldiers and sailors around young officers looking for a command. Shi Jichang argues that the Zheng should take and hold the ports across the strait. Officers who served farther south argue that the squadron should go where it can win secure bases and fly the Zheng banner over more of the southern sea lanes. Which officers lead the group determines where it pushes the court to act.

The Zheng survival, Shi Lang’s continued loyalty, the Manila Compact, He Shen’s dealings with Tungning, and the 1836 court figures are alternate history. Historical anchors include [the Zheng campaigns and Qing conquest of Taiwan](https://jsis.washington.edu/taiwan/2025/07/19/the-real-reason-for-how-taiwan-became-chinese/), [Zhu Shugui’s service with the Zheng and arrival in Taiwan](https://collections.nmth.gov.tw/CollectionContent.aspx?a=132&rno=2011.012.0233.0009), [the defeat at Hoton Nor](https://www.battle-of-qurman.com.cn/literature/Hwang_2014.pdf), [Qianlong’s Ten Great Campaigns](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/qing-military-institutions-and-their-effects-on-government-economy-and-society-16401800/E1FDAA965C5B67C12274B2B3408AE206), and [Jiaqing’s purge of He Shen in 1799](https://en.pgm.org.cn/2022-06/06/c_759201.htm).

## After 1836: The bargain across the strait

If the Heavenly Kingdom rises while the court is still debating the mainland, the Zheng must answer the same question they faced when Wu Sangui rebelled: how much can a common enemy buy? In the [Taiping bargain](design/journals/08-taiping-rebellion.md), the court negotiates as Tungning. It offers arms and help from its fleet in return for recognition of its coastal claims, the land Taiping can cede, and trading rights. With Fujian, Guangdong, and Zhejiang in hand, it can instead offer to supply two army depots and keep a squadron on station in exchange for a share of the southern interior. The territory Taiping started with stays outside the bargain. The partners can help each other at Shanghai long before either court agrees on who should rule China.

A [first foothold](design/journals/06-mainland-reconquest.md) puts that question to Taiwan itself. Moving the court across the strait leaves the island asking what it will govern for itself, while the southern courts that dealt with a maritime kingdom now have to bargain with a mainland power. If Qing gives way to rival governors, [reunification](design/journals/07-reunification-of-china.md) depends on which of them will accept a place in the new order and which insist on ruling China themselves.

The first county registers show what victory costs. Taiwan has too few clerks to take over the mainland’s offices. Local magistrates know who owns the fields and who fled, and can persuade villages to accept the new government if they keep their posts. Xu’s commissioners can insist on a single system of accounts where there are enough clerks to keep them. In the worst hit counties, the court can [open relief offices](design/journals/06-mainland-reconquest.md#state-decrees) and begin by giving people a reason to trust it.

Every inland victory gives the old estates another argument for [restoring the Ming](design/journals/05-constitutional-settlement.md#ming-restoration). If they cannot even defend Taiwan, the argument falls apart. The question of who pays for the rites and who attends them is part of the court’s dispute over faith. The compromise puts a Zhu on the throne for state ceremonies and a Zheng in charge of the cabinet. Victory over the Dutch or rule over Manila makes the opposite case: a dynasty that built and defended a maritime kingdom can keep its own crown.
